Four cases from the floor

Burger Day, 11:00

≈ £11k

International Burger Day 2025 at Shake Shack Birmingham: buy one, get one free on every burger. By 10:30 a queue had formed behind the shutter.

At 11:00, as the doors opened, a delivery worth about £4,000 arrived at the back door. Chilled and frozen stock could not sit outside. Expo is the station where finished orders are checked and handed out. The two team members slowest there went to put the delivery away, given 15 to 20 minutes, and Expo became my station, with 15 to 20 orders on the screen.

Most of the team could work most stations, so people came across to help whenever their own station was under control. At 11:30 I moved to the dining room, where one colleague was covering 120 to 150 seats alone.

Once service was back on time, I came off the stations completely: restocking from the back, checking temperatures and talking to guests. After 15:00 we covered breaks two at a time. The day took about £11,000, with no friction anywhere in the team.

Served withAn area manager once put it plainly: if the manager has to go on station, he is not managing well. Cross-training is what lets the manager step back on the worst day.

Food knowledge, taught in three passes

3 passes

At Shake Shack and at Fresh Bake, new items reached the team the same way, so that nobody learnt a dish by serving it wrong.

  1. Learn it. The brand's online learning: recipes, procedures, quality checks, and what each item should look and taste like.
  2. Make it. Next morning, before opening, each person made the item themselves and we rated it together. We moved on only when every item met the standard.
  3. Ask about it. On shift I asked quick questions at random. A right answer earned a Caught Doing Right card, the recognition card we gave for anything done well.

At Fresh Bake, the learning plan we co-developed opened day one with a tasting session and a short quiz on how each person learns best: by watching, listening or doing.

Underneath the three passes sat one standard for the whole team. Checking with the Manager on Duty is always welcome. Serving a guest an item that isn't right is never acceptable.

The team held to it carefully.

A slow start, then Employee of the Month

2 months

A new team member at Birmingham was far slower than everyone else, and I was ready to end his probation. Before deciding, I talked to him after a shift, as a colleague first.

He told me about a condition I hadn't known about. Then he showed me how to hand tasks to him so he could do them well, and we rebuilt his shifts around his strengths.

Two months later he was Employee of the Month. When an area manager later wanted him dismissed over one wrong order for a senior visitor, I asked for a month. Dismissal never came up again.

We are still in touch a year after I left.

The stock count that only looked bad

under £40

One stock count at Birmingham came in about £4,000 below what the system expected. The quick reaction would have been to treat it as a loss and tighten everything.

I compared the biggest gaps with the previous count instead. Most of the swing reversed an over-count from the period before, when the stock system had been down.

Across the two periods the net gap was under £40. That pointed us at the one product line that was genuinely out, chicken bites, instead of the whole kitchen.

The analysis went into the cost report I wrote for the site twice a month.

Six habits, every service

Every 1.5–2 hours

Walk the travel path

The same 13 checkpoints, from the front door to the staff room, so shortages and compliance slips show up early.

Before service

Brief the purpose

The team hears the day's goal, what each person is working on, and why each task is asked for. People work better when they are part of the plan.

Kitchen to floor

Check the pass

Temperature and presentation are checked where the kitchen hands food to the floor, before a plate goes out.

In the room

Touch every table

Managers and team visit tables, run food and offer refills. A host greets and thanks every guest at the door.

Daily

Close the feedback loop

Guest feedback read each day and shared in the briefing. Low scorers contacted to put it right. Complaints handled with the five A's: awareness, acknowledgement, apology, action and additional generosity. Regulars' names and orders remembered.

New tasks

Do it first, then coach

I do a new task myself and time it. Then I hand it over with a time limit and coaching, so the roster stays lean.

The first 90 days

Days 1–15

Learn

Get trained on the venue's way of doing things: the menu with the kitchen, the suppliers, the bookings pattern and the regulars. Note every bottleneck.

Days 16–30

Ask why

Understand why each process was chosen before changing any of it, from the team and the kitchen as well as the GM.

Days 31–60

Fix and test

Try new approaches on the bottlenecks, one at a time, and keep what the numbers and the guests support.

Days 61–90

Lock it in

Write down what works, train it, and hand it to the team so it runs on any shift.

Before you call

Why a Newcastle gastro pub?

A gastro pub sits where I do my best work. The food matters, the floor is busy, and labour, stock and guest scores all have to balance in the same week. The ad describes a venue where food and service are the point, and that is the restaurant I want to run.

What will I bring with me?
  • Rosters and forecasts. Built the Birmingham management roster before opening, set the daily and weekly sales forecasts, and planned labour, prep and stations to them.
  • Cost control. Weekly ordering to each supplier's cut-off, every delivery checked against the order, a cost report twice a month, challenges to delivery-platform refunds, and a review of the site P&L each period.
  • Food safety and audits. Co-developed the allergen and ingredient guide at Fresh Bake, ran the travel path, and presented the site on audit walk-rounds, including to senior visitors from the US. In March 2025 the NSF food-safety auditor named handwashing, cross-contamination control and temperature control as the site's three best practices.
  • People. Hiring through an in-store trial with a site tour, scored 1 to 5 on commercials, customer, people, and legal and standards, which I co-developed at Fresh Bake. Then inductions and probation reviews, employee-relations casework through a formal framework, the full payroll run in two businesses, and rosters for under-18 team members within their legal limits.
  • Openings. Two of them, Fresh Bake's launch store and Shake Shack Birmingham, from snagging to the first weeks of trading.
  • Reporting. A weekly commercial report for the area manager: sales against budget, average spend, labour, waste and complaints.
